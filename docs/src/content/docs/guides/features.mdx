This page covers what a [client](/guides/core-concepts/#clients) can do, how actors sign in and sign
out, and what happens to their accounts over time.

## Clients

### Registering a client

| How | Who | Approved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Added | A manager, in `/manage`. `/manage` shows the secret once. | Yes |
| [Handshake](/guides/connecting-via-sdk/#connecting-an-app) | A person approves the app at `/handshake`. The app's server then trades a one-time token at `/register` for its credentials. | Yes |
| [Declared in a file](/guides/self-hosting/#declaring-apps-in-a-file) | The operator, in the file named by `MASKS_CONFIG`. | Yes |
| [SAML application](/guides/sso-saml/#saml-applications) | A manager, in `/manage`. | Yes |
| [Enrolled](#enrolling-a-machine) | An approved app, for a machine of its own. | Yes |
| Self-registered | The app, over dynamic registration, within the tenant's [ceiling](/guides/core-concepts/#the-ceiling). | No |
| [Metadata document](#a-client-named-by-its-metadata-document) | The app. It uses the URL of a document that describes it as its `client_id`. | No |

Only an approved client may:

- hold a `masks:` scope
- sign in as itself
- receive a [delegation](/guides/connecting-via-sdk/#delegation)
- name `resources` it serves. A self-registered client that names `resources` is refused.

A client always grants its `required_scopes`. It grants its `allowed_scopes` when asked. A request
that names no scope gets all of them.

Archiving a client stops it from signing anyone in and revokes its live tokens. masks keeps its
record.

By default:

- An access token lasts an hour.
- A refresh token lasts 30 days unused, and each refresh starts the 30 days over. masks issues a
  refresh token only when the client is granted `offline_access`.

A client's **General** tab sets its own lifetimes:

- Access tokens from 1 minute to 1,440 minutes (a day).
- Refresh tokens from 1 day to 400 days. The manage API accepts as little as 5 minutes.

### Adding a known app

masks knows how to set up some apps. To add one:

1. Choose **Clients**, then **Add client**, then **A known product**.
2. Pick the app under **Product**.
3. Enter **Its address**.

masks then:

- registers it with the redirect URIs and scopes that app expects
- sets its address as the **Home page**, so it appears under **Your apps**
- lists each value to enter in the app, under the app's own name for it, including the client secret

| App | Needs beyond its address |
| --- | --- |
| Grafana, Nextcloud, Immich, Outline, Audiobookshelf, Portainer, Vaultwarden, Mealie, Open WebUI | Nothing |
| Gitea, Forgejo, Paperless-ngx, Proxmox VE, Jellyfin (SSO plugin) | **Name it uses for masks**. This is `masks` unless you change it. |
| Any app behind Caddy, Traefik, or nginx | Nothing. See [forward authentication](/guides/forward-auth/). |

Grafana, Nextcloud, Gitea, Forgejo, Paperless-ngx, Proxmox VE, Jellyfin, Mealie, and Open WebUI
read the [groups claim](#groups). In Grafana, Gitea, Jellyfin, Mealie, and Open WebUI, the settings
make the members of a group called `admins` the app's administrators. In every other app, choose
the administrators in the app.

### A client named by its metadata document

An app that has never registered can use a URL as its `client_id`. The URL points to a JSON document
the app publishes about itself. MCP clients work this way. masks fetches the document the first time
the URL arrives, and registers the client as self-registered.

```json
{
  "client_id": "https://app.example.com/oauth/client.json",
  "client_name": "Example App",
  "redirect_uris": ["https://app.example.com/callback"],
  "scope": "openid profile"
}
```

The URL:

- uses HTTPS
- has a path
- has no fragment, no username or password, and no `.` or `..` segments

The document:

- names its own URL as `client_id`
- is at most 5 kilobytes
- holds no `client_secret`, `client_secret_expires_at`, `registration_access_token`, or
  `registration_client_uri`

The client signs in with PKCE alone, or with `private_key_jwt`. PKCE (Proof Key for Code Exchange)
proves that the same client started and finished a sign-in, without a secret.

masks keeps a copy of the document:

- for as long as its `Cache-Control` header allows, from five minutes to a day
- for an hour when the header gives no `max-age`

When masks cannot fetch the document, or refuses it, the client cannot sign anyone in. masks tries
again after five minutes. A private address is refused, as for every
[call to another server](/guides/security/#calls-to-other-servers).

A manager can archive the client to refuse it.

Discovery is the document at `/.well-known/openid-configuration` that tells apps what masks
supports. It sets `client_id_metadata_document_supported` to `true` while the tenant allows dynamic
registration. With registration off, masks fetches no new documents. A copy it already holds works
until the copy expires.

### Pages on other sites

A page on another site can read discovery, the signing keys, and the answers from `/token`,
`/revoke`, `/userinfo`, and `/avatars`. Browsers send no cookies with these requests. A site with no
server can sign people in this way. See [static sites](/reference/javascript/static/).

### Client authentication

A client proves who it is at the token endpoint. Its `token_endpoint_auth_method` says how.

| `token_endpoint_auth_method` | |
| --- | --- |
| `client_secret_basic` | The secret in a Basic `Authorization` header. The default. |
| `client_secret_post` | The secret in the form body. |
| `private_key_jwt` | An assertion signed with the client's own key. masks checks it against the client's `jwks` or `jwks_uri`. |
| `none` | A public client. It proves itself with PKCE. |

- masks stores only a hash of a secret.
- A client registered with `client_secret_basic` or `client_secret_post` may send its secret either
  way.
- A secret client cannot send an assertion, and a `private_key_jwt` client cannot send a secret.
- A `private_key_jwt` assertion is accepted once, and expires within an hour.
- When an assertion uses a key masks does not know, masks fetches the `jwks_uri` again. A client can
  rotate its keys without telling masks.

### Consent

masks asks each actor once before a client receives anything. It asks again when:

- the client asks for more scopes or resources
- the client passes `prompt=consent`
- the client sends [authorization details](/guides/security/#rich-authorization-requests) the actor
  has not approved
- the sign-in is a [device sign-in](#device-sign-in)

Some rules change when masks asks:

- Approving a handshake is the consent of the actor who approved it, and of nobody else.
- A manager can turn off **Ask actors to consent** for an approved client, such as a first-party app.
  A self-registered client always asks.
- A manager can set **Consent lasts** on a client's **General** tab, from 1 to 400 days. The manage
  API accepts as little as 5 minutes. Once a consent is that old, masks asks the actor again, and a
  refresh fails with `invalid_grant`. Without a lifetime, a consent lasts until it is revoked.

### Subject identifiers

The `sub` claim is the identifier a client sees for an actor.

- A `public` client sees the actor's own identifier as `sub`.
- A `pairwise` client sees an identifier made for its sector. Two sectors cannot match up their
  actors.
- The sector is the host of the client's `sector_identifier_uri`, or the host of its redirect URIs
  when it has none.
- A `pairwise` client with redirect URIs on more than one host must register a
  `sector_identifier_uri` that lists them all.

### Signing in as itself

A service that acts for no person, such as a nightly job, uses the `client_credentials` grant:

```sh
curl -u "$CLIENT_ID:$CLIENT_SECRET" https://acme.auth.example.com/token \
  -d grant_type=client_credentials \
  -d scope="xixo:catalog:read"
```

A manager turns on **Signs in as itself (client_credentials)** for an approved client. The token:

- names the client as `sub`
- comes with no refresh token
- never holds a scope that acts for a person, such as `openid` or `masks:manage`

### Enrolling a machine

An approved app that has claimed a [namespace](/guides/core-concepts/#namespaces) can enroll machines
of its own. An example is an agent the app installs on each computer in a home. Nobody approves each
machine in a browser.

The app asks `/enrollments` for an initial access token. It authenticates the same way it does at the
token endpoint:

```sh
curl -u "$CLIENT_ID:$CLIENT_SECRET" https://acme.auth.example.com/enrollments \
  -d scope="xixo:agent" \
  -d client_name="nas" \
  -d expires_in=900
```

```json
{ "initial_access_token": "…", "expires_in": 900, "scope": "xixo:agent" }
```

- Every scope must fall under one of the app's namespaces.
- A prefix is refused, and so is any `masks:` scope.
- The token lasts from a minute to an hour. The default is 15 minutes.
- The token is spent the first time it registers a machine.
- Discovery publishes the endpoint as `enrollment_endpoint`.

The machine makes its own keys. It registers the public half, with the token as its bearer:

```sh
curl https://acme.auth.example.com/register \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $INITIAL_ACCESS_TOKEN" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"client_name": "nas", "jwks": {"keys": [ … ]}}'
```

masks answers with a new client. It is approved for the token's scopes and the app's resources. The
new client:

- is named by `client_name` in the registration, then the token's `client_name`, then "A machine"
- authenticates only with `private_key_jwt`, against those keys
- uses only the `client_credentials` grant
- gets access tokens that last 10 minutes
- binds every access token to a key with [DPoP](/guides/security/#binding-a-token-with-dpop), so a
  token works only for whoever holds that key

The answer holds a registration access token and no secret. The private key never leaves the
machine.

- A registration without `jwks`, or with a `jwks_uri`, is refused. The token stays unspent.
- A machine cannot change its own registration. masks refuses a `PUT` with `403`.
- A machine can remove itself with a `DELETE` to its `registration_client_uri`.

In the manage API, the new client names the app as `enrolledBy`. Archiving the app archives every
machine it enrolled and revokes their tokens.

To unenroll a machine it enrolled, the app sends a `DELETE` to the same endpoint. It authenticates the
same way:

```sh
curl -u "$CLIENT_ID:$CLIENT_SECRET" -X DELETE \
  https://acme.auth.example.com/enrollments/$MACHINE_CLIENT_ID
```

masks archives the machine, revokes every token it holds, and answers `204`. Asking again gets the
same answer. A client the app did not enroll is refused with `404`.

## Signing in

### Sign-in policies

A policy sets how actors sign in. A sign-in uses the first of these that exists and is not archived:

1. The [organization's](/guides/organizations/#policy) policy.
2. The client's policy.
3. The tenant's default policy.
4. The built-in policy.

Managers edit policies under **Settings**, then **Policies**, in `/manage`. A policy's form has
these parts:

| Part | Holds | Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Signup** | **Open**, which lets people create their own accounts. **Email domains** that signup and provider sign-in accept. **Confirmation** of a new account: **None**, **Emailed code**, **Emailed link**, or **Manager approval**. **Signup scopes**. **Hide who has an account**. **Browser check**. | **Open** off, any domain, **None**, the standard scopes, **Hide who has an account** off, **Standard** |
| **Account** | Whether an account has a **Nickname**, an **Email**, and a **Phone**: **Off**, **Optional**, or **Required**. **Confirmed email** and **Confirmed phone**, which ask an actor to confirm each one before they sign in. | **Nickname** **Optional**, **Email** **Required**, **Phone** **Off**, neither confirmed |
| **Sign-in** | The first factors. The minimum password length. **Refuse common passwords**, from a list of about 47,000. **Require a password at signup**, which stops a passkey alone. The providers to offer. | **Password**, **Passkey**, and **Provider**. 8 characters. **Refuse common passwords** on. **All providers**. |
| **Second factor** | The second factors an actor can set up. **Required for everyone, not just managers**. **Asked again at a sign-in to a client when the session did not use one**. | **Authenticator app**, **Passkey**, and **Backup codes**. Both switches off. |
| **Risk** | The scores that ask for a second factor or refuse a sign-in, and **Refuse passwords found in known breaches**. See [risky sign-ins](/guides/security/#risky-sign-ins). | **Never**, and breached passwords allowed |
| **Sessions** | See [how long a session lasts](#how-long-a-session-lasts). | 14 days, no idle limit |

- **Emailed code** and **Emailed link** confirmation, and **Hide who has an account**, need
  **Email** to be **Required**.
- **Hide who has an account** sends an email address a code before anything else. A browser already
  signed in to that account, or a device it trusts, skips the code.
- An organization's **Email domains** also refuse a sign-in to that organization from an address
  outside them.
- The manage API names each setting in the [`SignInPolicy`](/reference/manage/#signinpolicy) type.

A policy hides every first factor it does not list. Its second factors decide what an actor can set
up. A second factor an actor already has works under any policy.

The tenant's default policy always keeps a password or a passkey, so managers are never locked out.
Whatever the policy says, a manager must set up an authenticator app or a passkey as a second factor.

### Browser check at signup

The **Browser check** makes the browser solve a hashing puzzle before it can sign up. This makes
creating accounts in bulk slow and costly.

| Level | Time on a laptop |
| --- | --- |
| **Off** | None |
| **Light** | A fraction of a second |
| **Standard** | About a second |
| **Strong** | Several seconds |

- Nothing goes to another service.
- Without JavaScript the browser cannot solve the puzzle, so signup says it needs JavaScript.
- A puzzle lasts ten minutes, and is accepted once, for the sign-in it was made for.
- The first account on a tenant skips the check. Accepting an invitation does not ask for it.

### First factors

A first factor is how an actor first proves who they are.

| Factor | |
| --- | --- |
| **Password** | Checked against the policy's minimum length and the common passwords. |
| **Passkey** | WebAuthn. It also counts as a second factor when the authenticator verified the person. |
| **Provider** | An account at Google, Okta, a SAML provider, and others. See [single sign-on and SAML](/guides/sso-saml/). |
| **Emailed code** | A six-digit code sent to the account's email address, with no password. See [signing in with an emailed code](#signing-in-with-an-emailed-code). |

When the policy offers passkeys at signup and the browser can make one:

- The credentials step offers **A passkey** beside **A password**, and starts on the passkey.
- An account made with a passkey has no password.
- The passkey counts as both factors. This includes the second factor a manager or a policy
  requires.
- The person can set a password later from **Password** on their account page, or through a reset
  link sent to their email address.

When the browser supports passkey autofill, the sign-in field lists the actor's passkeys among its
suggestions. Choosing one signs in without typing a nickname or address.

masks names each passkey after its authenticator, using the
[FIDO Metadata Service](https://fidoalliance.org/metadata/). It marks a passkey whose model the
service reports as compromised. It refuses a sign-in from a passkey that looks copied.

### Signing in with an emailed code

A policy that offers **Emailed code** shows **Email me a code instead** on the password step. When
the policy offers no password, it shows only **Email me a code**.

The tenant's default policy keeps a password or a passkey. To offer a code alone, set it on a policy
for the apps that want it.

- The code has six digits, lasts 10 minutes, and allows five attempts.
- Another code can be sent after 30 seconds, within the
  [rate limit](/reference/environment/#rate-limits).
- An email address or nickname with no account gets the same screen and no mail, so the form does
  not show who has an account. An account that has reached its limit of codes gets the same.
- An account that has not accepted its invitation gets no code.
- An account with a password and an unconfirmed email address gets no code. This stops someone from
  taking over an address they do not own.
- Signing in with a code confirms the account's email address.
- Under a policy that hides who has an account, the actor proves the address first.
- The code proves the inbox. It never counts as a second factor, and the email second factor is not
  offered after it.

### Second factors

A second factor is a second proof, asked for after the first factor. The `amr` claim lists how the
actor signed in.

| Factor | | `amr` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Authenticator app** | A time-based code the app shows. | `otp` |
| **Passkey** | A passkey whose authenticator verified the person. | `user` |
| **Backup codes** | One-time codes, for when the other factors are lost. | `otp` |
| **Email codes** | A six-digit code sent to a confirmed email address. | `otp` |
| **SMS codes** | A six-digit code sent by text message through the tenant's SMS service. | `sms` |
| **Approval from a trusted device** | The actor enters a code shown on the new sign-in into the account page of a device they trusted. | |

A person turns email or SMS codes on from their account page. This needs:

- a confirmed email address or phone number
- a policy that offers them, unless the person is a manager
- a mail or SMS service the tenant can send with

Once on, a code is asked for at every sign-in, under any policy. Changing the address or phone
number turns its codes off. So does a password reset through an emailed link, for email codes. An
email code does not confirm a sign-in whose first factor was a provider.

A person adds or removes an authenticator app on their account page. Once they have an
authenticator app or a passkey, they can make a new set of backup codes there.

- Each of these, and turning email or SMS codes on, asks for a sign-in within the last 15 minutes.
- Adding the first authenticator app also makes backup codes when the account has none.
- New backup codes replace the old set.
- Removing the authenticator app drops the backup codes, unless a passkey remains.

After any second factor except a backup code, the actor can ask masks to trust the device. masks
then skips the second factor on that device for 30 days, or until the device is signed out.

An actor who gets past the first factor but cannot use any second factor can ask a manager for help.

1. The actor chooses **I cannot use any of these**. When none of their second factors works in this
   sign-in, the button reads **Ask a manager for help**.
2. masks records `recovery.requested`.
3. masks mails every manager holding `masks:manage` or `masks:manage:support` who has a confirmed
   email address.
4. masks sends the actor a [security email](/guides/security/#security-emails), so someone who stole
   only the password gets noticed.

Asking again within a day does nothing.

In `/manage`, the overview counts who asked. The **Asking for help** filter on **Actors** lists them.
On the actor's page, a manager chooses one of these:

- **Dismiss** drops the request.
- **Reset second factors** removes the authenticator app, backup codes, passkeys, and email and SMS
  codes. It forgets every trusted device and signs the actor out everywhere. Their next sign-in asks
  for a new second factor when the policy requires one.

### Device sign-in

A device that cannot run a browser, such as a television or a command-line tool, uses the device
grant:

1. The device asks `/device_authorization` for a code.
2. It shows the person the code and the `/device` address.
3. It polls `/token` until the person approves on another device.

| Poll response | Do |
| --- | --- |
| `authorization_pending` | Keep polling. |
| `slow_down` | Add five seconds to the interval. |
| `access_denied` | Stop. The person declined. |
| `expired_token` | Request a new code. Codes last 15 minutes. |

A user code is eight letters with no vowels, such as `BCDF-GHJK`. masks ignores case and dashes.

### Languages

Sign-in, consent, the account pages, mail, and `/manage` are in English, French, Spanish, and
German. masks picks the language in this order:

1. The first of the four languages that an app names in `ui_locales`.
2. The first of the four languages that the browser's `Accept-Language` header asks for.
3. English.

Dates and times follow the same language.

## Signing out

### Signing out from an app

An app sends the browser to `/logout`. Discovery lists this as the `end_session_endpoint`.

```
GET https://acme.auth.example.com/logout
  ?id_token_hint=eyJ…
  &post_logout_redirect_uri=https://app.example.com/signed-out
```

- `post_logout_redirect_uri` must be one the client registered.
- A `GET` without an `id_token_hint` for the signed-in actor shows a confirmation screen first. A link
  on another site cannot sign someone out.

Each server SDK protects the app's own sign-out too. It refuses a sign-out from another site with
`403`. A `GET` from a browser that sends no `Sec-Fetch-Site` header, such as Safari before 16.4, gets
a page with a **Sign out** button that posts the sign-out. See signing out for [Rails](/reference/ruby/#signing-out),
[JavaScript](/reference/javascript/#signing-out), [Python](/reference/python/#signing-out), and
[Go](/reference/go/#signing-out).

### Telling other apps

When a session ends, masks tells every app the actor used in that session. This is back-channel
logout.

- masks posts a signed `logout+jwt` to each app's `backchannel_logout_uri`.
- masks tries each delivery five times. It records a delivery that still fails in the actor's
  activity.
- masks never posts to a private address unless the operator allows it. See
  [calls to other servers](/guides/security/#calls-to-other-servers).
- SAML applications hear through [single logout](/guides/sso-saml/#single-logout).

### Sessions and devices

- A **session** is one signed-in browser.
- A **device** is the browser itself. masks knows it by a cookie that lasts 400 days.

| Action | Ends |
| --- | --- |
| Revoke a session | That sign-in. |
| Sign a device out | Every session and token on it, and its trusted second factor. |
| Sign an actor out | Every session and refresh token on every device, and every trusted second factor. |
| Block a device | Every request from it, before any password is checked. |

`/manage` also lists devices that never finished signing in, under **Never signed in**, so you can
still block them.

### How long a session lasts

A session lasts 14 days by default. A sign-in policy changes this under **Sessions**.

| Setting | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Last** … **after signing in** | A session started under the policy expires this long after signing in. A client covered by the policy asks the actor to sign in again in any older session. |
| **End after** … **idle** | A session started under the policy ends after this long with no request. masks records `session.expired`. A client covered by the policy asks the actor to sign in again after a gap this long. |

- `/manage` offers lifetimes from an hour to 90 days, and idle limits from 15 minutes to a day. The
  manage API accepts anything from 5 minutes to 400 days.
- The idle limit must be shorter than the lifetime.
- A refresh token issued in a session that either setting limits stops working when the session ends.
  This includes when the actor signs out.

## Organizations

An **organization** is a customer inside a tenant. Each member holds a role there. An app that asks
for the `organization` scope signs an actor in as a member of one organization. See
[organizations](/guides/organizations/).

## Groups

A **group** is a named set of actors in a tenant, such as `admins` or `family`. An actor can be in any
number of groups.

To set up groups, a manager holding `masks:manage:security` or `masks:manage`:

1. Adds groups under **Settings**, then **General**, then **Groups**.
2. Puts actors in them on each actor's **Access** tab.

An app that asks for the `groups` scope receives a `groups` claim. The claim is in the ID token and in
the userinfo answer. It lists:

- the key of each group the actor is in
- each organization role they hold, written as `key:role`

```json
{ "groups": ["acme:owner", "admins", "family"] }
```

Every actor may grant the `groups` scope, whatever their own scopes are. Apps that map roles from a
claim read this one. In Grafana, for example:

```ini
[auth.generic_oauth]
scopes = openid profile email groups
role_attribute_path = contains(groups[*], 'admins') && 'Admin' || 'Viewer'
```

### Who may sign in to an app

On a client's **Access** tab, **Who may sign in** limits the client to some actors. Tick groups, or
type **Organization roles** written as `key:role`, separated by commas or spaces.

- Anyone else who signs in to it is sent back to the app with `access_denied`. The refusal appears in
  their activity.
- Taking someone out of the group ends their next refresh with `invalid_grant`.
- A client with nothing ticked lets everyone in.

## Accounts

### Suspending and deleting

A manager suspends, restores, or deletes an actor in `/manage`. A directory does the same over
[SCIM](/guides/sso-saml/#provisioning).

- Suspending signs the actor out everywhere. It refuses every sign-in until a manager or the
  directory restores them.
- Deleting signs the actor out everywhere, and tells apps that registered for back-channel logout. It
  removes their passkeys, consents, connected accounts, and photo.
- Nobody can suspend or delete the last manager.

### Idle accounts

A tenant can suspend accounts nobody uses, and later delete them. To set this up in `/manage`:

1. Open **Settings**, then **Policies**, then **Auto-deletion**.
2. Set a period for **Suspend**, **Delete**, or both. Leave either at **Never** to turn it off.

When both are set, deleting comes later than suspending.

- Signing in counts as using the account. So does an app refreshing a token for the actor.
- masks emails a warning 30 days before each step. It takes the step no sooner than 30 days after the
  warning.
- Turning the settings on or changing them gives every account a fresh 30 days.
- Signing in before a suspension keeps the account. Once it is suspended, only a manager can restore
  it. The warning before deletion says so.
- With **Suspend** set, masks deletes only accounts suspended for being idle.
- With **Suspend** at **Never**, masks deletes an idle account directly.
- An account that a manager or a directory suspended stays until they decide.
- An account with no confirmed email address cannot be warned. It follows the same schedule.
- masks never touches the last manager, accounts provisioned over SCIM, or invitations nobody has
  accepted.
- masks records each warning, suspension, and deletion as an event.

### Your apps

The top of a person's account page shows **Your apps**. It lists the apps the person may sign in to:

- every approved client that has a **Home page**
- every SAML application that can start a sign-in itself

Each one opens the app, or starts the SAML sign-in through masks. A client nobody approved never
appears.

### Changing your own details

A person changes their name and nickname under **Name** on their account page.

To change an email address or phone number:

1. Enter the new email address or phone number.
2. Enter the six-digit code masks sends to it.

The change takes effect once the code is entered.

- Whether another account already uses the address shows only after the code is entered.
- The change asks for a sign-in within the last 15 minutes.
- The change turns off sign-in codes sent to the old address.
- When the old email address was confirmed, masks mails it about the change.
- A person can also remove their phone number, unless the sign-in policy requires one.

An account that signs in only with a passkey or another account can set a password from **Password**,
after a recent sign-in. Setting it signs out every other session.

Accounts a directory provisions over SCIM keep the name and addresses the directory gives them.

When a manager changes someone's confirmed email address in `/manage`, masks mails the old address

### Downloading your own account

A person downloads everything masks holds about them as a JSON file. They choose **Download** under
**Download your account** on their account page.

The file holds:

- their profile
- which factors they use
- their passkeys
- their organizations and roles
- their connected accounts, and what each app was allowed to do with them
- the apps they consented to
- their devices
- every event about them that the tenant still keeps

It holds no password hash, authenticator secret, backup code, token, or key.

- masks records each download as `account.exported`.
- masks emails the person, unless they turned off the **Account** group under
  [**Emails we send you**](/guides/security/#security-emails).
- A person can download their account five times an hour.

### Deleting your own account

A person deletes their own account at the bottom of their account page. To confirm, they type its
nickname, or its email address when it has no nickname.

The last manager cannot delete their own account. Neither can an account provisioned over SCIM.

Downloading and deleting both ask a person who signed in more than 15 minutes ago to sign in again
first.
